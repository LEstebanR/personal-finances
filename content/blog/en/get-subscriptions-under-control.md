No single subscription looks expensive on its own. $8/month for streaming, $10 for music, $5 for cloud storage — each one, by itself, is a reasonable call. The problem shows up when they're all added together: most people who seriously review their subscriptions for the first time are surprised by how much they're paying in total, every month, without ever having decided that all at once.

There's a name for this: it's called subscription creep — the quiet, one-at-a-time growth of subscription spending until the total stops being small.

## Why it's so easy to lose track of the total

Subscriptions don't compete for attention at the moment of payment. A credit card gets charged automatically every month, no confirmation asked. There's never a conscious "am I still paying for this?" moment — it just keeps getting charged until someone actively cancels it, and canceling always takes more effort than just letting it ride.

On top of that, every subscription has its own renewal date, in a different app, with receipts that arrive separately. There's no single place where the total is visible.

## Step one is just listing them all

Nothing needs to get cancelled yet — the first step is simply writing down every active subscription with its amount and how often it renews (monthly, yearly, or otherwise). Often it's enough to scroll through the last two months of card transactions to find recurring charges that aren't even remembered anymore.

## Not every subscription renews the same way

Some are monthly, some are yearly, and some — like certain discounted plans billed every few weeks — don't fit neatly into either. If wherever they're tracked only supports "monthly" or "yearly," those end up missing or mis-logged, and they're exactly the ones that get forgotten most easily, since their charge date isn't the same every month.

## Review every subscription once a year, not just when it annoys you

Most people only review their subscriptions after the bank flags an unexpected charge. An annual review — honestly asking "do I still use this?" for each one — keeps "just in case" from quietly turning into a permanent fixed expense nobody actually chose to keep.
